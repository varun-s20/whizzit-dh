### Intermittent problems usually start underneath

Cabling faults rarely announce themselves. They surface as a call that drops once a day, a machine that negotiates at a tenth of its speed, or a camera that goes offline when the heating runs. Because the symptoms look like software, teams often spend weeks in the wrong place before anyone tests the cable.
- [CTA] 01Certification is not the same as it works today
A tester that lights up green confirms continuity. Certification measures the things that actually degrade performance: crosstalk, return loss, attenuation and length, against the standard for the category installed. It is also the only evidence that the installation met specification on the day, which matters when a fault appears two years later and nobody can agree what was installed.
- [CTA] 02Category choice is a decision about the next decade
Cabling outlives several generations of the equipment plugged into it, so the sensible question is not what today’s switches need but what the building will need before it is next refitted. The cost difference between categories is small next to the cost of pulling cable through an occupied building a second time.
- [CTA] 03Comms rooms fail for physical reasons
Heat, dust and improvisation account for a large share of hardware failures. A rack in a cupboard with no ventilation will shorten the life of everything in it, and a rack with no patching discipline turns a five-minute change into an afternoon. Ventilation, power, containment and labelling are not tidiness for its own sake; they decide how long a fault takes to find.
- [CTA] 04Labelling and records are the part that gets skipped
An unlabelled patch field means every future change begins with tracing. A documented schedule mapping outlet to patch panel to switch port turns that into a two-minute job. It is the cheapest thing to do during installation and the most expensive to reconstruct afterwards.

### The disruptive part is access, not the cable

Most of the work in an occupied building is about getting to places: ceilings, risers, and rooms that can only be entered outside business hours. Agreeing routes, containment and access windows before starting is what keeps an installation from becoming a series of interruptions, and it is the part most often underestimated when a date is promised.
