### What we account for in Leicester

Leicester’s economy leans towards garment and food manufacturing and, because of where it sits on the motorway network, distribution and logistics. Both run on shift patterns, which is the detail that most standard IT support arrangements ignore.
- [CTA] 01Cover has to match a shift, not an office day
If production starts at six and a second shift runs into the evening, a fault at seven in the morning is not an out-of-hours nicety. It is worth being explicit about which hours are genuinely covered rather than discovering the boundary during an incident.
- [CTA] 02Warehouses defeat office wireless design
Racking, ceiling height and moving vehicles break coverage in ways a floorplan never shows. Scanners and terminals need a survey of the actual building, and often more access points in fewer places than expected.
- [CTA] 03Seasonal peaks arrive as headcount
Order peaks bring temporary staff who need accounts on day one and no access on the day they leave. Automating that cycle is the difference between clean permissions and a directory full of dormant logins.
- [CTA] 04Close enough that a same-day visit is realistic
Leicester is among the more accessible locations from our Stevenage base, so where something genuinely needs hands on hardware a same-working-day attendance is a reasonable expectation rather than an aspiration. That is worth knowing if you run production equipment that cannot wait.
- [CTA] 05A workforce that is often multilingual and shift-based
Manufacturing and distribution employers in the city frequently run diverse, multilingual teams on shared terminals across shifts. That pushes the design towards individual accounts on shared devices, clear session handling and security training that works for people who do not sit at a desk all day.
- [CTA] 06The stock system, not email, is the one that must not stop
Where distribution and light manufacturing are involved, priorities usually need reordering. Email being slow is an irritation; the warehouse system being unavailable stops picking, dispatch and invoicing at once. That should be reflected in what is monitored most closely, what is covered by the highest response priority, and what has a tested fallback. It is worth writing down which systems genuinely halt trading, because the answer is rarely the one people assume.
- [CTA] 07Taking card payments pulls part of the network into scope
Retail and wholesale operations that take card payments inherit obligations about how those systems are separated from everything else. The practical work is keeping payment devices off the same flat network as the office machines and the warehouse kit, so the questions a bank or an assessor asks have a clear answer. Doing that when the network is next touched costs very little; retrofitting segmentation across a live site is a different job entirely.
