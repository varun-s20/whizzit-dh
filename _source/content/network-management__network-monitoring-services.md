### An alert nobody acts on is just a log entry

Monitoring tools are easy to buy and easy to ignore. The difference between a dashboard and a service is whether an alert reaches a person who is expected to do something about it, within a time you have agreed. Everything else is decoration.
- [CTA] 01Thresholds tuned to the site, not to the vendor default
Out of the box, most monitoring alerts on values that mean nothing in your environment, and the team learns within a fortnight to dismiss them. Once that habit forms, the genuine alert is dismissed too. Tuning thresholds to what is normal for each device, and deliberately alerting on less, is what keeps the alerts worth reading.
- [CTA] 02The signals that actually precede an outage
Hardware rarely fails without warning. Rising interface errors on a switch port, a firewall session table filling steadily, a failing power supply in a resilient pair, disk latency creeping up, an access point rebooting nightly: each of these is visible days ahead if something is watching. That window is the entire argument for monitoring.
- [CTA] 03Capacity questions need history, not a snapshot
When someone asks whether the connection needs upgrading or whether the switch stack will cope with thirty more staff, the honest answer requires months of trend data. Monitoring that only shows the present moment can describe a problem but cannot support a purchase decision, which is where most of the budget conversations actually happen.
- [CTA] 04Knowing what is connected in the first place
Monitoring depends on an accurate inventory, and inventories drift. Devices get added during a project and never recorded, or replaced without the record being updated. Reconciling what is discovered on the network against what is documented is unglamorous and routinely finds equipment nobody had accounted for.
