### What we account for in Bradford

Bradford has an unusually high proportion of owner-managed businesses and a manufacturing base rooted in textiles and food production. Many of them also operate across the Leeds corridor, which quietly turns a single-site business into a two-site one.
- [CTA] 01Budgets that expect the spend to be defensible
Owner-managed firms tend to ask what a line item prevents before approving it, which is a fair question. We would rather cost the things that stop the business working and be honest that the rest can wait than present a package where nothing is optional.
- [CTA] 02Production and office IT sharing one network
In smaller manufacturers the office switch and the machine on the floor are frequently the same network, because it grew that way. Separating them is usually the single highest-value piece of work available, and it rarely requires new hardware.
- [CTA] 03Working across Bradford and Leeds as one business
Staff moving between sites in the same week need their files, phone extension and permissions to follow them. That is an identity and cloud question rather than a networking one, and it is cheaper to solve properly than to work around.
- [CTA] 04Onsite attendance is a planned journey, not a call-out
Our engineers are based in Stevenage and travel north, so a Bradford visit is arranged rather than immediate. In practice most faults are resolved remotely long before a visit would arrive, and we would rather be straight about the travel than describe ourselves as local.
- [CTA] 05Older building stock shapes what is physically possible
Much of Bradford’s commercial space is in converted mill and older industrial buildings, where thick stone walls, long cable runs and limited riser access constrain what a network can do. Wireless surveys in these buildings routinely produce different answers from the floorplan, and cabling work is often the unglamorous prerequisite to everything else.
- [CTA] 06Machines usually outlive the software that drives them
A press or a production line bought fifteen years ago is often still perfectly serviceable, and still controlled by a PC running something no longer supported. Replacing the machine to solve a software problem is not a sensible answer. Isolating that PC on its own network segment, removing its need for internet access, and backing up its configuration is the pragmatic one. The goal is that an unsupported system cannot be reached from the office network and cannot reach anything else.
- [CTA] 07Backups that only cover the office miss the production data
Office files are usually protected because they live in a cloud platform somebody manages. Production data frequently is not: machine configurations, job files, quality records and the small local database on the shop floor PC that nobody thinks of as a server. Those are the things that make a rebuild slow when hardware fails. Listing what a full recovery would actually require, then checking each item is covered, is a job worth doing once and reviewing yearly.
